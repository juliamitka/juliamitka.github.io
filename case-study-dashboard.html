Dashboard design for a B2B customer panel

Transforming a passive service catalog into a dashboard that helps users understand their company's situation and decide what to do next.

Role Product Designer
Scope IA · Usability testing · UI
Type Enterprise / B2B
Product Web application

Overview

Product

A web application for a business information exchange platform, used by companies to verify contractors, monitor company health, and manage receivables.

Project

Redesign of the homepage - the first screen after login - from a static grid of service tiles into a decision-support dashboard.

Research

Customer support insights shaped the initial structure, and the second iteration was validated through moderated usability testing with 6 users - business owners and finance professionals.

Challenge

The previous homepage functioned as a product catalog - a grid of tiles leading to services also available in the side menu. It duplicated navigation without adding informational value.

The problem was not only related to the UI. The homepage did not answer the questions users had when logging in: What is the current status of my company? How many services do I still have available? Have any new events appeared? What should I do next?

The biggest challenge was not the lack of data or functionality, but prioritization - what the user should see first in order to understand the situation and make a decision.

Main page - Before

Duplicated entry points

All tiles led to the same services already available in the side menu.

No clear next step

No guidance on what action made sense given the user's current situation.

No information priority

All tiles had equal visual weight - no way to identify what mattered most.

Design challenge

How can we transform the homepage from a service catalog into a dashboard that gives users a quick overview of their company's situation and guides them toward the right action?

The dashboard needed to address problems visible from both the users' and the business perspective: low homepage usability, recurring support questions, and underuse of services available in the panel.

Without reliable homepage analytics available at this stage, customer support became the strongest source of user insight. Their recurring conversations with users revealed repeated friction around package limits, reports, service differences, alerts, and unclear next steps. Key insight

Users didn't need more features. They needed context and guidance.

The problem wasn't a lack of functionality - it was the lack of a clear structure explaining what users had available, what required attention, and what action made sense next.

Dashboard Logic

The key shift was moving from "let's show users the available products" toward "let's answer the questions users have when they log in." Users had access to many services, but lacked context: when, why, and in what order to use them.

" How many services can I still use? "

Your packageAvailable checks, monitoring services, quick access to invoices

" Is my company in a good situation? "

Your company statusMost important report data - shown without downloading a full report

" What is happening around my company? "

Your statisticsKey indicators of activity around the company

" What can I do now? "

What can you do now?Recommended actions grouped by user goals

Design decision

The dashboard was designed as a sequence of answers to user needs, not as an exposition of all available services.

Usability Testing

The goal-based navigation addressed some of the original problems, but the dashboard still contained a lot of information, so I tested V2 with users before refining it further.

UX research plan

I structured the study using Iga Mościchowska's UX research planning canvas.

01Project

What product, and at what stage?

Product: customer panel homepage.

Timing: after the second iteration (V2), before the final version.

The panel combines company status, statistics, package limits, and action shortcuts. The test focuses on information priorities and service findability.

What could disrupt the test or its interpretation?

  • Placeholder data may make statistics harder to interpret.

Does the V2 dashboard help users quickly assess their company's situation, understand key data, and find the right action?

  2. Statistics - Do they understand the metrics and see their practical value?
  3. Navigation - Do they intuitively start verifying a contractor?
  4. Receivables - Do they recognize the right path for handling an overdue payment?

08Hypotheses to verify

Assumptions before the study

  • H1 Multiple sections make it hard to notice information that requires action.
  • H2 Some statistics are unclear or don't support decisions.
  • H3 Grouping services by goal is intuitive, but there are too many cards.
Key tasks

  • Task 1 - Assessing company status

    You've just logged into the platform at the start of your workday. Before moving on to your other tasks, you want to get a quick overview of your company's current situation and make sure nothing important needs your attention.

    Use the information available on this page to assess how your company is doing and determine whether there is anything you should follow up on.

  • Task 2 - Checking a potential business partner

    Your company is considering working with a new contractor who has offered attractive terms for a long-term partnership. You've never worked with this business before, and you'd like to know more about its financial reliability before making a decision.

    Starting from this page, find a way to check the company before agreeing to the partnership.

  • Task 3 - Handling an overdue payment

    One of your regular customers hasn't paid an invoice for PLN 8,000, even though the payment deadline passed two weeks ago. You've checked your records and confirmed that the payment hasn't arrived.

    You'd like to address the outstanding payment. Use the platform to start the action you consider most appropriate for this situation.

Key findings

Information overload

Multiple sections competed for attention.

Package visibility

Named among the most useful information on the dashboard.

Security score

An unexplained 50% score caused stress and frustration.

Too many statistics

Only reports, monitoring entities, and events felt actionable.

Too many actions

Goal-based columns worked, but long cards and labels slowed scanning.

01

Information overload made prioritization difficult

Participants were generally able to locate relevant information, but 3 of 6 hesitated before identifying what deserved their attention. Multiple sections competed for attention, making it harder to distinguish important company-related updates from secondary information.

Design response

Strengthened the visual hierarchy, giving company status greater prominence and reducing competing elements.

02

Not all statistics provided meaningful value

While participants appreciated having an overview of their company's activity, 3 of 6 struggled to interpret some statistics, and one couldn't explain what they meant without help. Downloaded reports, entities monitoring the company, and company-related events were perceived as the most actionable.

Design response

Simplified the statistics section to focus on these three key indicators, removing redundant data and metrics with limited decision-making value.

03

Goal-based navigation worked, but too many actions created friction

In Task 2, all 6 participants found the right category on their first attempt, confirming that grouping actions by goal rather than product name worked. However, the number of available options, lengthy descriptions, and additional labels made the section more difficult to scan - half of the participants compared several similar cards before choosing.

Design response

Preserved the goal-based structure while simplifying action cards, removing less relevant and external services, and making the primary actions easier to identify.

04

Package information was among the most valued

In the opening and closing questions, two participants spontaneously named package limits - alongside company status - as one of the first things they check after logging in and one of the most useful pieces of information on the dashboard.

Confirmed

Kept package information high in the dashboard hierarchy, maintaining quick access without requiring additional navigation.

05

The security score caused anxiety instead of guidance

The prototype showed a security level of 50%, and participants reacted to it with stress, worry, and frustration. It wasn't clear where the value came from or how it was calculated. Company data showed that most clients would score below 50%, so the effect in production would likely be even stronger. At the same time, the score was mainly raised by paid services offered outside the customer panel.

Design response

Removed the security score from the dashboard. Company status is now communicated through report data with a clear meaning instead of an unexplained score.

Key takeaway

All three hypotheses were confirmed. V2 was moving in the right direction. Users understood the goal-based structure but still struggled to prioritize, so the final iteration focused on hierarchy rather than new features.

Final Iteration

V3 Focused dashboard

Based on testing insights, the final version focused on stronger prioritization: fewer competing elements, no security score, a simpler statistics section, and a clearer focus on company status and the user's next step.

Final UI

1
Package visibility

Number of remaining checks and monitoring services shown immediately after login.

Why

Package limits were one of the most common reasons for contacting support. In testing, participants named them among the most useful information on the dashboard.

2
Company status snapshot

Key data from the company report - credibility, credit limit, situation in registers - without opening a full report.

Why

Complex data is presented through short messages, statuses, and colors - users get interpretation before detail.

3
Focused statistics

Downloaded reports, entities monitoring the company, and company-related events.

Why

Fewer, clearer indicators strengthen the overall hierarchy.

4
Guided actions

The product catalog was replaced by "What can you do now?" - recommended actions grouped by goal: taking care of your company, checking a contractor, or recovering receivables.

Why

Users didn't always know which service to use. Goal-based language - not service names - makes the path from intent to action clear.

UI Direction

The dashboard was also an opportunity to start organizing the UI layer of a legacy product. In a system built around complex financial data, consistent typography, spacing, and components reduce cognitive load and help users scan faster.

Consistent typography

Unified text styles and usage rules for headings, body text, labels, and helper descriptions - replacing the mix of fonts used across the panel.

Spacing system

Defined rules between sections, cards, and elements inside components to make the dashboard easier to scan visually.

Accessible primary color

New primary color meeting WCAG contrast requirements - the existing one failed for interactive elements throughout the panel.

More whitespace

Increased space between modules and inside cards - improving data readability and making the dense financial content easier to parse quickly.

Clearer CTA and status hierarchy

Defined styles for buttons, links, and status labels so users can distinguish information from actions faster - especially important in a context where users often receive alert-type data and need to understand what requires action vs. what is just informational.

Reflection & Learnings

What worked well
Support teams are an underrated research source

Recurring questions revealed real user problems faster than waiting for analytics would have.

Testing V2 before polishing it paid off

The final iteration could focus on what users actually struggled with - such as the security score and the number of actions - instead of assumptions.

User questions became a decision-making framework

Structuring the dashboard around questions users had after logging in helped guide iteration decisions. For each element, I could ask: does this answer a real user need, or does it only add more information?

What I would improve
Test the final version as well

The usability sessions validated V2, but the changes made in the final iteration were not tested. I would repeat the same task-based sessions on the final version - assessing company status, checking a contractor, and handling an overdue payment - to confirm improvements in task efficiency and usability.

Define success criteria earlier

I would also define measurable success criteria earlier, such as fewer support questions, higher usage of key services, and faster access to company status information. This would help connect design decisions to business outcomes from the start.
